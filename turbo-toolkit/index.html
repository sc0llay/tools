How to read this

The operating line is the answer. It is where your engine actually sits on the map, swept from idle to redline. A good match keeps that line inside the efficiency islands, off the surge line at low rpm, and short of the choke wall at redline. Drag the boost, VE and A/R controls and watch it move.

Compressor sizing is set by inducer diameter — choke flow scales with inducer area, so a 54 mm wheel walls out near 62 lb/min and a 71 mm near 107 lb/min. Exducer diameter sets tip speed and therefore how much pressure ratio the wheel can make before it hits its shaft speed limit.

Turbine sizing is set by wheel exducer and housing A/R together — they set swallowing capacity, which sets turbine inlet pressure P3. Watch the backpressure trace, turbine inlet pressure divided by boost pressure, both as gauge readings: 1:1 is ideal, below about 1.5 the turbine is comfortable, above roughly 2.0 the engine is pumping against itself, scavenging suffers and knock margin drops even though the compressor looks fine. That is the single most common mismatch: right compressor, undersized turbine.

Wastegate position tells you whether the turbine has authority. A gate pinned shut at redline means the turbine is out of capacity and boost is falling off; a gate more than about 70% open at low rpm means the turbine is oversized for the housing and you have thrown away response.

Arrangement changes the sizing problem, not just the total. A parallel twin splits the engine between two turbos, so each one is matched to half the displacement — the map and every per-turbo readout show one turbo's share, while power and total flow are for the whole engine. That is why a parallel-twin 4.0 V8 puts each turbo at exactly the same point on the map as a single-turbo 2.0.

Cylinder count and scroll type set pulse separation. Each scroll is fed by cylinders ÷ (turbos × scrolls), so pulses arrive every 720° divided by that. Blowdown lasts roughly 240°, so once the firing gap drops inside that figure the pulses run into one another: the turbine sees flattened, less useful energy and the manifold fights itself. Eight cylinders into one open scroll fire every 90°; the same engine twin-scrolled or split across two turbos fires every 180°. The model applies this as a turbine-efficiency multiplier and a manifold interference term, both deliberately conservative — real divided-housing gains depend heavily on manifold design.

Fuel is modelled by chemistry, not by a power multiplier. Blends are specified by volume the way pump E-numbers are, converted to mass fraction by density, and stoichiometric ratio, energy content and latent heat all blend from there — E85 lands at 9.81:1 and 29.2 MJ/kg against published 9.76 and 29.2. Set mixture with lambda so it stays comparable across fuels. Crucially, evaporative charge cooling is not applied to manifold density: fuel evaporates in the port and cylinder, downstream of where manifold air density is set. It buys knock resistance and a modest trapped-mass gain instead. That is why swapping to E85 at fixed boost barely moves power here — the real gain is that it lets you run more boost, which you can see by raising the boost slider and watching the knock row.

The knock row is indicative only. It is a simple octane-requirement correlation in boost, charge temperature and compression ratio. It knows nothing about your timing, cam profile, chamber design, coolant temperature or fuel distribution, and it credits only the first 45 K of evaporative cooling because past that the correlation is extrapolating. Use it to compare fuels against each other, never as a calibration target.

Model & data provenance

Wheel geometry, shaft speed limits and peak efficiencies come from published Garrett G-series catalogue pages and Xona Rotor / dealer spec sheets. Entries marked (est) are interpolated within a frame family where the manufacturer does not publish inducer/exducer dimensions — treat those as indicative, not as datasheet values.

Xona's UHF turbine wheels carry a distinct aero credit, not just different dimensions: Xona markets the UHF generation specifically on flowing more for a given physical size and holding efficiency over a wider speed range than the wheel it replaced. That is a directional, published characteristic rather than a reverse-engineered coefficient, so it is modelled as a deliberately modest +6% swallowing capacity and a 15% flatter blade-speed-ratio efficiency curve versus the Garrett-calibrated baseline — shown as "UHF aero" in the turbine panel whenever a Xona wheel is selected. It is not modelled as faster spool: that would be an inertia effect, and this is a steady-state solver with no rotating inertia in it at all.

Housing A/R and wastegate port size are treated as your own hardware choices, independent of which wheel is selected — swapping the turbine wheel (Garrett ↔ Xona, or between frame sizes) keeps your current A/R and gate size rather than resetting to a "typical" value, so you can compare wheel technology at a fixed housing. A/R is clamped to the newly selected wheel's available housing range if your current value falls outside it.

The physics is a steady-state 1-D match: a backswept-impeller Euler head model (ψ = σ − k·φ) with an asymmetric off-design efficiency penalty and an inducer-area choke limit, closed against a turbine swallowing-capacity curve and a blade-speed-ratio efficiency curve, balanced on shaft power with a wastegate bypass orifice. It reproduces published map shape and flow capacity well and is meant for comparing candidates against each other. It is not a substitute for the manufacturer's measured map, and it models no transient inertia — spool-up rate is inferred from turbine capacity, not simulated.
